Does this read human, or does it read AI?

// a real audit, not a vibe check

This site is built with AI, and I'd rather say that plainly than have someone else notice it first. So instead of just hoping the writing holds up, I counted it: every case study on this site, run against the actual patterns that make AI writing recognizable, with the real numbers and the real offending sentences below. Same discipline as the rest of this portfolio — show the gaps, not just the polish.

Verdict

Not there yet. The facts are real; the sentences were AI-shaped.

The substance — the metrics, the trade-offs, the architecture decisions — is specific and genuine throughout. The prose that carries it, though, had clear, measurable machine-writing fingerprints: one construction repeated dozens of times, a heading vocabulary that converged across unrelated case studies, and an em-dash habit far outside normal human range. Below is exactly what I found, and what I'm doing about it.

"Skills Demonstrated" appears as a heading 12 times. "Reusable Insight" appears 10 times. "The System," "Key Concepts," and "Open Questions" each appear 7 times. A person writing 29 case studies over months doesn't converge on the same closing heading that often — a template applied in one sitting does.

What Was Actually Doing It

Four specific patterns, pulled with real examples, not a general impression.

The em-dash reflex — 580 uses. Used for asides, contrasts, and appositives almost interchangeably, at roughly 4–8× a typical human professional writer's rate.

a live, interactive dashboard — not just a write-up
delay minutes are drawn from a gamma distribution, not a normal one — real delays are right-skewed

The "not just X, it's Y" elevation — 45 uses. A reflex for making something sound more significant than its plain description. Reads as insight the first time, formula by the fifth.

"My Trips" functions as a lifecycle retention engine, not just a post-booking utility tab
every recommendation above has a cost, not just an upside

The "X isn't Y" hard contrast — 23 uses. Close cousin of the pattern above — declaring what a thing definitely isn't before saying what it is.

"a PRD isn't a spec doc. It's a decision memo in disguise."

Template convergence across case studies — 32% repeat rate. Different products, different industries, the same section skeleton — the B2B "study guide" entries all run The System → Key Concepts → Open Questions → Skills Demonstrated, and nearly every case study closes on a section literally called Reusable Insight.

Why this happened: most of this site was drafted by one AI system across a small number of long sessions, not accumulated the way a person actually writes a portfolio — one case study at a time, over months. Consistency of voice is usually a good thing. Here it's consistency of which model wrote it, and that's the real tell, more than any single sentence.

What Already Reads Human

Fairness matters here too — not everything on this site has this problem.

Reads human

Specific, checkable numbers

"₹50,000 Cr+ deposit book," "FD booking turnaround from 7 days to 1," "Heroes Award," "integrating with Finacle via REST APIs" — real, verifiable, oddly specific facts. AI filler tends toward round, vague claims; this doesn't.

Reads human

The visible "TODO" gaps

AI optimizing for looking finished fakes completeness. This site does the opposite — over a hundred honestly-labeled gaps, still marked incomplete instead of quietly papered over. That restraint reads as trustworthy, not machine-polished.

Reads human

The recommendations section

Literally other people's words, quoted from LinkedIn — the single most human text on the whole site, because it's the only text nobody here wrote.

Fix Plan

Concrete, not "try to sound more human."

  1. Cut the em-dash count by half — a mechanical pass forcing every third or fourth one into a period, a comma, or a parenthetical instead. The goal isn't zero, it's breaking the reflex.
  2. Cap "not just X" and "X isn't Y" at one use per case study. Both are fine occasionally; the problem was 45 and 23 uses in the same voice.
  3. Let headings be bespoke per case study instead of picked from a six-heading rotation — if two case studies would naturally get the same heading, that's a sign to write toward what's actually different about each one.
  4. Write the remaining personal categories (Career Lessons, Decision Journal, Google PM Interview, Personal Growth) starting from my own raw words, not a clean draft to approve. Lightly-edited real voice beats a polished AI draft every time — this is the highest-leverage fix on the list.

Counted against the live site content, updated as the fixes above actually ship — not a one-time badge.
