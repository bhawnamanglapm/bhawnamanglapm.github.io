T1

Flight Booking Product Teardown

CIRCLES method HEART framework (Google) OKR-aligned recommendation RICE + MoSCoW Business model & risk analysis

C — Comprehend the Situation

The clarifying questions I'd ask before touching the product, the way a Google PM interview expects: which platform (web vs. app — both), domestic or international (domestic only), and an existing product or a new one (existing, mature product). Scope: MakeMyTrip's flight search → booking flow specifically, not hotels or holidays — narrower on purpose, so the analysis goes deep on one flow instead of shallow across three.

Business model: the flight vertical monetizes through airline commissions/overrides, a convenience fee layered on the base fare, and ancillary revenue (seat selection, insurance, baggage). That matters here specifically — add-on pre-selection and the late-stage fee reveal found below aren't accidental UX oversights, they're revenue mechanisms. Any recommendation that removes them has a real, quantifiable cost, not just an upside; see Risks & Trade-offs below.

North Star Metric (company level): completed bookings per unique searcher — normalizes for the high browsing-only traffic that never intends to book. This teardown is scoped to moving that metric one funnel stage at a time, starting with the stage that leaks hardest.

I — Identify the Customer

Three personas span the segmentation axes that actually predict booking behavior — price sensitivity, time pressure, and party complexity — not demographic axes like age or city, which don't:

  • "Value Rakesh" (28, IT professional, 4–5 domestic trips/yr) — price-sensitive, compares 3+ apps before booking.
  • "Business Priya" (34, consulting manager) — books last-minute, values reliability and rebooking flexibility over price.
  • "Family Planner Anita" (41) — books for 4+ pax, needs group-seat clarity and abandons on unclear total-fare breakdowns.

R — Report the Customer's Needs

Journey: Home search widget → results list (filters/sort) → fare review (baggage/meal add-ons) → traveller details → payment → confirmation + "My Trips." Logged-out users are funnelled into account creation at payment, not upfront — reduces search friction but adds a late-stage form-fill tax.

Retention loop: "My Trips" functions as a lifecycle retention engine, not just a post-booking utility tab — it's the reason a user reopens the app at all after booking (rebooking, cancellation, web check-in). That's why Business Priya's reliance on it post-confirmation (see the funnel map below) is a commercial signal, not just a UX nicety — it's the retention mechanism the acquisition funnel below feeds into.

Needs uncovered, mapped to the journey above:

  • Trust/transparency need — fare inflation between search-result price and fare-review price (taxes/fees revealed late) erodes trust; add-on pre-selection (insurance, seat) defaults to "on," reading as a dark pattern at checkout.
  • Time-efficiency need — filter fatigue (12+ dimensions, no smart default) and no persistent price comparison across dates force users to manually reopen search in a new tab.
  • Clarity/control need — baggage policy buried a tap deep, a top cause of post-booking support contact.

Funnel: Search → Results → Fare Review → Traveller Info → Payment → Confirmation. The biggest modeled drop-off sits between Results and Fare Review (price-shock from add-on fees — modeled at ~38% conversion in the Booking Funnel Analysis (T8)), with a second leak between Traveller Info and Payment (form fatigue + OTP retry friction).

Competitors: ixigo surfaces the convenience fee earlier than MakeMyTrip and leads with "flight confirm guarantee" trust messaging; Booking.com's flights product (smaller India share) shows all-in price at search. MakeMyTrip's "My Trips" is the strongest post-booking lifecycle surface of the three.

C — Cut Through Prioritization

Of the three personas, I'd prioritize Value Rakesh's need first. Price-sensitive comparison shoppers are both the largest segment of OTA search traffic and the segment most exposed to the single biggest funnel leak above — the fare-review price shock hits a price-anchored searcher hardest. Business Priya's reliability need and Family Planner Anita's group-booking complexity are real, but lower-frequency — worth a later cycle, not this one. Everything below is scoped to fixing Rakesh's need first.

L — List Solutions

  1. All-in fare display at the results level — kill the late-stage price reveal.
  2. Opt-in (not opt-out) add-ons at fare review.
  3. Persistent "compare dates" strip on results instead of reopening search.
  4. Baggage/meal policy inline on the fare card, not a tap away.
  5. One-tap "hold my seat 15 min" for indecisive high-intent users.
  6. Smart filter defaults ranked by price + duration + layover, not price alone.
  7. Payment retry with saved card + biometric re-auth to cut payment-stage drop-off.
  8. Proactive delay-risk badge on results (feeds the Flight Delay Dashboard (T3)).
  9. Group-booking mode for 4+ pax with a shared itinerary link.
  10. Post-booking "manage trip" nudges (web check-in, gate-change alerts) via push.

Risks & Trade-offs

Every recommendation above has a cost, not just an upside — naming it here, not after launch:

  • All-in fare display — the honest total can look higher than a competitor's headline price at the results stage, hurting click-through from price-comparison meta-search and paid search ads. The fix that builds trust downstream may cost impressions upstream.
  • Opt-in add-ons — directly reduces ancillary revenue per booking (insurance/seat attach rate). This is the business-model trade-off named in Comprehend, made concrete — it needs a revenue-per-booking floor agreed with Finance before shipping, not just a UX sign-off.
  • Baggage/meal policy inline — minor engineering cost, no material revenue risk. The lowest-risk item on the list, consistent with its RICE ranking.
  • Delay-risk badge — risks eroding trust in a different direction if the underlying prediction (see the Flight Delay Dashboard, T3) isn't well-calibrated. A wrong badge is worse than no badge.

Mitigation: ship all-in pricing and opt-in add-ons behind the A/B test already scoped in the Booking Funnel Analysis (T8), with revenue-per-booking as a guardrail metric alongside conversion — not conversion alone. A win on Fare Review conversion that quietly craters ancillary revenue isn't actually a win.

S — Summarize Recommendation

Ship all-in fare display and opt-in add-ons first — RICE 13.5 and 14.4, the two highest-scored and lowest-effort items on the list. Together they remove the exact price-shock that costs the funnel its single biggest leak, for the segment (Rakesh) chosen in the Cut step above. Everything else on this list sequences behind those two.

Success Metrics — the HEART Framework

Using Google's own HEART framework (Rodden et al.) instead of a single catch-all "engagement" number:

  • Happiness — CSAT specifically on "pricing clarity" (post-booking survey); NPS delta before/after the all-in pricing rollout.
  • Engagement — searches per session; % of sessions using the new compare-dates strip.
  • Adoption — % of eligible searchers who see and interact with the all-in fare display / opt-in add-on toggle in month one.
  • Retention — repeat booking rate within 90 days, segmented by whether a user's first booking hit the old opt-out add-on flow or the new opt-in one.
  • Task success — Results→Fare Review conversion (the primary funnel-leak metric, North Star for this cycle), search-to-confirmation completion rate, time-to-book.

Tied to an OKR

Objective: Remove price-shock and trust friction from fare review for price-sensitive searchers.

  • KR1: Increase Results→Fare Review conversion from the ~38% modeled baseline (see Booking Funnel Analysis, T8) to 50%+ within one quarter.
  • KR2: Reduce "hidden fees" CSAT verbatims by 50%.
  • KR3: Ship both P1/Must recommendations this quarter, validated against the A/B hypotheses already scoped in T8.

TL;DR

"Wayfare AI" — a conversational trip-planning assistant that turns "plan me a trip" into a bookable, weather- and budget-aware itinerary in one session. MVP scope: single-destination domestic trips only, 3–7 days. Ships itinerary generation, a budget optimizer, and weather-aware sequencing; explicitly does not attempt multi-city routing, group itineraries, or live disruption reflow in v1 — see Goals & Non-Goals below for why.

Problem Statement

Planning a multi-day trip today means 8–12 open tabs (flights, hotels, blogs, weather, maps) and hours of manual cross-referencing. Users abandon planning mid-way and either overpay on a generic package or under-plan and lose time on-trip re-deciding.

Goals & Non-Goals

Goals (v1):

  • G1: Cut time-to-first-itinerary to under 90 seconds for a first-time planner.
  • G2: Convert at least one booked leg per generated itinerary, at a target rate set post-launch (the North Star, below).
  • G3: Let a budget-conscious traveller swap any single item without manually recalculating the rest of the plan.

Non-Goals (explicitly out of scope for v1):

  • Multi-city or multi-destination routing.
  • Shared or group itineraries.
  • Live disruption reflow — needs a flight-status API integration not yet built; sequenced for v2 once MVP itinerary quality is validated, not because it's unimportant (it's Reactive Rhea's entire use case, see below).
  • Replacing a travel agent for complex international multi-leg trips — this is a single-destination planning aid, not a full-service booking replacement.

Users & Use Cases

  • "First-timer Farah" — plans 1 international trip/yr, wants 2–3 easy choices, not raw options.
  • "Optimizer Omar" — books 6+ trips/yr, wants full control and only trusts AI suggestions he can see the reasoning behind.
  • "Reactive Rhea" — mid-trip, needs the plan to adapt live when a flight delays or weather turns (her use case is explicitly a Non-Goal for v1 — see above).

User stories:

  • As a first-time planner, I want to type "5 days in Vietnam under ₹60k" and get a full day-by-day itinerary, so I don't have to research every leg myself.
  • As a budget-conscious traveller, I want to see how each day's plan affects my total spend, so I can swap a paid activity for a free one without recalculating manually.
  • As a returning user, I want the assistant to remember my dietary and pace preferences, so I don't re-explain them every trip.
  • As a skeptical planner, I want to see why a restaurant or attraction was recommended, so I can trust or override the suggestion.

Design — Key Flows

  • Intent capture — single chat-style input + 3 quick-pick chips (budget band, trip length, vibe), not a 12-field form.
  • Itinerary review — day-by-day vertical timeline, each activity swappable via a "Why this?" expand showing rating/distance/price reasoning.
  • Budget bar — persistent sticky bar showing spend-to-date vs. target, updating live as items are swapped.
  • Book/hold — itinerary converts to a bookable checklist (flight, hotel, key activities) with per-leg deep links.

Success Metrics

North Star: itineraries that convert to at least one booked leg. Supporting: time-to-first-itinerary (target <90s, ties to G1), swap rate per itinerary (a trust signal on first-pass suggestion quality — high swap rate means the first draft isn't landing), budget-target adherence rate, D7 return rate for pre-trip re-edits.

Launch Plan

  • Phase 1 (MVP): single-destination domestic trips, 3–7 days. Itinerary generation + budget optimizer + weather sequencing only — the three P0 requirements.
  • Phase 2: multi-city routing + persistent preference memory, gated on Phase 1 hitting its swap-rate and time-to-itinerary targets — no point routing multiple cities if the single-city itinerary isn't trusted yet.
  • Phase 3: live disruption reflow (Reactive Rhea's use case), gated on a flight-status API integration that doesn't exist yet.

Risks & Open Questions

Risk: hallucinated venue details (wrong hours, permanently closed) need a live data-freshness check, not just training-time accuracy. Recommendation logic must stay auditable and explainable so it doesn't over-fit to popular/sponsored listings (same scoring discipline as the Hotel Recommendation Engine project).

Open questions this doc doesn't resolve:

  • Should the budget optimizer be allowed to suggest a different destination than requested if it saves >30% of budget — or does that override user intent in a way that breaks trust?
  • What's the fallback UX when no itinerary can be generated within the stated budget — degrade gracefully with a "closest match," or refuse and ask the user to adjust?
  • Does surfacing restaurant/attraction rankings require a sponsored-placement disclosure, and who owns that legal review before launch?

Reusable Insight

The hard PM problem isn't generating an itinerary — it's designing the trust layer (the "why this?" reasoning, swap-not-regenerate interaction) that lets a user hand over planning without feeling like they lost control of the trip.

T3

Flight Delay Prediction Dashboard

8,000 flights analyzed Self-contained HTML/JS dashboard CVD-validated palette Full dark-mode support

The Problem

An OTA or airline can't act on delay risk it hasn't aggregated. A single flight-status page tells one traveller their flight is late; nothing rolls that up into the pattern a PM actually needs — which routes are structurally unreliable, which airlines run tighter operations, and when in the calendar or the day risk spikes. This project builds that rollup, answering the three questions a travel-tech PM would actually be asked before shipping something like the "delay-risk badge" recommended in the Flight Booking Teardown (T1): which routes delay most, how airlines differ, and how season and time of day shape delay risk.

My Approach

The honest constraint first: this was built in an environment that can't reach DGCA, US BTS, or Kaggle to pull a real historical dataset. Rather than present scraped-looking numbers that weren't actually real, I built a modeled dataset instead and said so directly on the dashboard — 8,000 flights generated from parameters I chose and documented: fictional airline names (so no delay number is ever attributed to a real, identifiable carrier), real Indian city-pair routes (airport congestion at Delhi/Mumbai/ Bengaluru and effects like North Indian winter fog are public aviation-industry knowledge, not a claim about any company), and additive delay effects for route congestion, season, and time-of-day. Delay minutes are drawn from a gamma distribution, not a normal one — real delays are right-skewed with a hard floor at zero, and gamma is bounded at zero and long-tailed by construction, which a normal distribution isn't. The first pass came out at a 48.9% on-time rate — implausibly low for any real airline — so I re-tuned the distribution parameters until the fleet-wide numbers matched a plausible real-world range, while keeping the underlying patterns (winter fog, monsoon, evening cascade) intact. Full calculation, every parameter, and the reasoning behind each one are documented in the dashboard's own Methodology section, summarized here in full.

Methodology — the calculation, step by step

For every one of the 8,000 simulated flights:

  • Draw a base delay from Gamma(shape=1.3, scale=3.2) — mean ≈ 4.2 minutes, right-skewed.
  • Multiply by that flight's airline reliability index (a single fixed number per airline, see table below) — the only airline-level effect in the model, kept deliberately simple so differences between the fictional carriers stay modest rather than dramatic.
  • Multiply by a time-of-day multiplier (0.90 Early Morning → 1.55 Night) — models how delays cascade later into an operating day.
  • Add a fixed route congestion term (see table below), scaled by 0.35 — a stand-in for how much busier Delhi/Mumbai/Bengaluru are than smaller airports.
  • If the route is monsoon-sensitive and the month falls in Jun–Sep, add an extra Gamma(1.6, 5.0) term (mean ≈ 8 min).
  • If the route is fog-sensitive and the month is Dec/Jan, add an extra Gamma(1.8, 6.0) term (mean ≈ 10.8 min), amplified 1.3× for Early Morning departures — fog is worst at dawn.
  • Cancellation is decided separately, as its own probability: a 0.6% base rate, +1.8pp if monsoon-exposed that month, +2.4pp if fog-exposed that month. If cancelled, delay is zeroed — a cancellation is a different outcome from "a very late flight," not the same thing at the extreme end.
  • A flight counts as delayed if its final delay exceeds 15 minutes (the standard on-time threshold) and it wasn't cancelled.

Why these specific numbers: not derived from any real source — chosen, then iterated. The first draft produced an implausible 48.9% on-time rate; I scaled the base gamma parameters and congestion multiplier down until the fleet-wide rate landed at 84.4% — inside a plausible real-world range — while preserving the relative shape of every effect (which season is worst, which route is worst, night vs. morning). What this deliberately doesn't model: real distributional parameters fit from a published dataset (none was reachable from this environment), or flight-to-flight correlation (a delayed inbound aircraft delaying its own next outbound leg) — a real airline operations model would need that, and it's a materially harder problem than this exercise set out to solve.

The Analysis

With the dataset built, I grouped it four ways — by route, by airline, by season, and by time of day — computing average delay, the share of flights delayed over 15 minutes, and cancellation rate for each. A single "worst route" or "worst season" number hides the risk that actually matters operationally, which is where two dimensions compound — so I cross-tabbed the top 8 routes against all 5 seasons into a heatmap instead of stopping at one-dimensional rankings.

Key Findings

  • 84.4% on-time fleet-wide, 9.2min average delay, 1.1% cancellation rate.
  • DEL-GOI is the single worst route (12.6min avg, 34% delayed >15min) — the only route in the model that's both winter-fog- and monsoon-sensitive, so it takes a hit twice a year instead of once.
  • Winter is the worst season by a wide margin (11.9min avg vs. 7.5min in Spring/Post-Monsoon) — every Delhi-origin route in the heatmap spikes in December–January.
  • Delays compound through the day — Night departures average 11.0min vs. 8.3min in the Early Morning, consistent with how real airline operations cascade delays leg-to-leg.
  • Airline-level spread is real but secondary — the gap between the most and least reliable modeled carrier (7.6–10.7min) is smaller than the gap between Winter and Spring (7.5–11.9min). Route and season explain more than carrier choice does.

Recommendations

What a PM would actually do with this, if it were live data instead of modeled data:

  • Surface delay risk during booking, not after. Feed a route + season + time-of-day risk score into the delay-risk badge recommended in the Flight Booking Teardown, so a traveller sees it before they book, not after they're already delayed.
  • Pad schedule buffers seasonally, not year-round. A flat on-time SLA misses that Delhi-origin routes need a materially wider buffer specifically in December–January, not every month.
  • Treat Night departures as an upstream ops problem. Since delay compounds through the day in this model, the fix is earlier-leg reliability, not just padding the last flight of the day.
  • Re-run this against real DGCA/BTS data before shipping anything downstream of it. This dashboard's job was to prove the analysis and the product judgment, not to replace a real data pipeline.

Reusable Insight

A delay dashboard's real product risk isn't the chart — it's presenting modeled or incomplete data as if it were ground truth. The discipline that matters here is the same one from the AI Trip Planner's guardrails: say what's real, say what's modeled, and never let a polished visualization imply more certainty than the underlying data supports.

T5

Customer Support AI Copilot

Agentic AI, not just Q&A 3 core conversation flows Escalation & guardrail architecture Deflection vs. safety trade-off

The Problem

The support queries that pile up fastest at an OTA — "cancel my flight," "how much baggage can I bring," "can I change the travel date" — are also the most repetitive and rule-governed. A human agent resolving them one ticket at a time is expensive and slow at OTA volumes; a plain FAQ chatbot that only answers questions can't actually act on a booking. The gap this project scopes is the one in between: an assistant that can both answer and execute — cancel a booking, initiate a refund, apply a fare rule — which is a materially different (and riskier) design problem than a Q&A bot, because it can now make a costly, sometimes irreversible mistake on its own.

Scope & Non-Goals

In scope: cancellations, refund recommendations, baggage-allowance questions, and date/name booking changes — the four query types responsible for most repetitive support volume. Explicitly routed to a human, never handled autonomously: visa legal advice (a compliance and liability question, not a support one — the bot can point to the airline/embassy's own documentation but never advise), disputes and fraud claims, and any refund above a fixed autonomous-action ceiling (see Guardrails below).

Where Today's Industry Falls Short

Before designing the flows, it's worth naming the pattern this design is deliberately not repeating. Travel and OTA support bots in production today tend to fail in one of four recurring ways — this isn't a claim about any specific company's product, it's the pattern visible across the category:

  • Answer-only, can't act — most bots can tell you the cancellation policy but can't actually cancel anything; the moment a user needs an action taken, they're back in a queue for a human, which defeats the point of automating the repetitive cases in the first place.
  • Black-box numbers — the few bots that do quote a refund or fee rarely show the rule behind it, so a user has no way to check it's right, which is the same trust erosion the fare-shock problem in the Flight Booking Teardown (T1) causes at the booking stage.
  • Silent or absent confirmation — bots that can act either skip a confirmation step entirely (risky) or are so conservative they add friction to low-risk actions too (annoying) — few calibrate the gate to the actual risk of the specific action, the way Flow 1 vs. Flow 2 do below.
  • Context loss at handoff — "please repeat your issue" after being passed to a human is one of the most common support complaints in the category; the bot's understanding of the problem evaporates the moment escalation happens.

The wireframes below (Screens 1–4) are the direct, one-to-one answer to these four gaps, not generic chat-UI decoration — each screen is annotated with the specific failure mode it closes.

Flow 2 — Baggage Policy Question (lowest-risk flow)

The contrast case: a pure information lookup with no action taken and nothing to confirm, so it needs none of Flow 1's guardrails.

  • User: "How much check-in baggage do I get on my booking?"
  • Agent (intent: baggage_query, 0.99 confidence) → tool call: fetch fare rules for the booking's specific fare class → "Your Economy Saver fare includes 15kg check-in. Extra baggage is ₹500/kg if you add it now, or ₹800/kg at the airport — want me to add extra baggage to this booking?"

Fully autonomous end-to-end — answered and resolved the likely follow-up (add baggage) without a human, because nothing here is irreversible or high-value enough to need a confirmation gate.

Flow 3 — Booking Date Change (medium-risk flow)

Sits between the two above: it's an action (not just information) but a correctable one, so it gets a lighter version of Flow 1's gate — confirm the fare difference, but no separate human-escalation trigger unless the fare-difference lookup itself fails. Intent: change_booking_date → tool call: check seat availability + fare difference on new date → agent states the fare difference and change fee → single confirmation → executes change_booking() and emails the updated ticket.

Agent Architecture

Four layers, in order: (1) Intent router — classifies the incoming message into one of the in-scope intents above, or "unclassified" → human. (2) Tool-calling layer — each intent maps to a fixed set of callable tools (PNR lookup, refund_policy_engine, fare-difference calculator, cancel_booking, issue_refund, change_booking) — the agent can only call tools on this allow-list, it cannot invent new actions. (3) Confirmation gate — any tool call that is irreversible or moves money (cancel_booking, issue_refund) requires an explicit user confirmation turn before executing, never fires on the first pass. (4) Escalation router — triggers on low intent-confidence (<0.85), a tool call failing twice, explicit user frustration signals, or any query touching a hard non-goal (visa advice, disputes) — handed to a human agent with the full conversation transcript attached, not restarted from zero.

Guardrails

This is the part of the design that actually differentiates a support copilot from a support chatbot — and the part a scope doc for an agent that can take real actions can't skip:

  • Autonomous-action ceiling — the agent may autonomously execute cancellations/refunds up to a fixed rupee ceiling; above it, the same flow runs but ends in a human-approval step instead of auto-executing.
  • Confirmation before irreversible actions — never optional, shown in Flow 1 above; a wrong autonomous cancellation is far more costly to trust than a wrong autonomous baggage-fee quote.
  • No autonomous visa/legal advice — the intent router treats this as a hard non-goal, not a confidence threshold — it escalates every time, regardless of how confident the model is in an answer.
  • Full audit trail — every tool call the agent executes (not just conversations) is logged with the triggering intent, confidence score, and outcome, reviewable by support ops — the same operational-audit discipline as the PCMS payout reconciliation work (see Technical Execution, J11).

Evaluation Approach

Deflection rate alone is a misleading headline metric here — a bot can "deflect" a ticket by giving an answer that satisfies the user in the moment but is wrong, which shows up as a good deflection number and a bad outcome. Evaluation needs three lenses, not one: resolution accuracy (sampled human audit of closed bot-handled tickets, not just the bot's own confidence score), wrong-action rate — the safety metric, tracking autonomous actions a human reviewer would have executed differently, weighted far more heavily than any convenience metric — and escalation-appropriateness, checking the router escalates when it should as often as it deflects when it shouldn't.

MetricWhat it's really measuringWhy it's on this list
Containment rate% of in-scope tickets resolved without a humanThe headline efficiency number — but never read alone (see below)
Wrong-action rateAutonomous actions a human audit would reverse or flagThe safety guardrail metric — this one has veto power over containment rate

Business Impact (modeled, not measured)

Illustrative, not a real support-cost figure: if cancellation, baggage, and date-change queries together are a modeled ~35% of ticket volume, and the copilot fully contains half of those (the baggage-query type especially, since it needs no guardrail and resolves in one turn), that's roughly a sixth of total support volume handled at near-zero marginal cost instead of a human agent's fully-loaded cost-per-ticket. The number that actually matters for a launch decision, though, is the wrong-action rate at that containment level — a model here would need real historical ticket data to size properly, which is exactly why this stays a scope doc and not a "built" project, unlike the modeled-but-labeled dataset in the Flight Delay Dashboard (T3).

Risks

The risk that actually matters isn't "the bot gives a bad answer" — a Q&A chatbot has that risk too, and it's recoverable. It's the bot takes a bad action: an autonomous cancellation or refund that shouldn't have happened is real money and a damaged booking, not just an unhelpful chat log. Every guardrail above exists to keep that specific failure mode's blast radius small and recoverable (confirmation gates, action ceilings, audit trails) rather than trying to prevent it outright, which no confidence threshold can fully guarantee.

Reusable Insight

The same lesson as the ChatGPT Memory Feature teardown (Feature Reverse Engineering, J2) shows up again here in a higher-stakes form: the hard PM problem in agentic AI isn't language understanding or intent classification — those are largely solved. It's designing the guardrail and escalation layer that keeps the agent's inevitable mistakes cheap and reversible instead of expensive and permanent.

T8

Booking Funnel Analysis

Funnel scope
OTA flight booking funnel: Search → Results → Details (fare review) → Traveller Info → Payment → Confirmation.
Biggest drop-off
Results → Details (modeled ~62% loss) — the fare-review price reveal is the single largest funnel leak. Users who arrive price-anchored from the results page bounce once taxes, fees, and add-ons inflate the total.
Root-cause reasoning
1. Anchoring bias — once a user sees a low headline fare, any upward revision reads as deception, regardless of accuracy.
2. Add-on defaults — pre-selected insurance/seat fees compound the price-shock instead of being clearly separable.
3. No fare-lock — an indecisive user has no reason to stay on the page vs. reopening a new tab to compare.
Improvement ideas
1. Show all-in price (base + tax + fees) at the results stage, even at the cost of a slightly less eye-catching headline number.
2. Make add-ons opt-in with one clear toggle, not a pre-ticked line item.
3. Add a 10-minute fare-lock CTA on the details page for indecisive users.
4. Autofill/remember traveller info for repeat and return-trip bookings.
5. Auto-retry payment with the saved method before falling back to full re-entry on a gateway timeout.
A/B test hypotheses
1. All-in pricing: showing all-in fare at results increases Results→Details conversion by removing price-shock, possibly at a small cost to search-engine click-through.
2. Opt-in add-ons: switching seat/insurance from pre-selected to opt-in increases Details→Traveller Info conversion, even if average order value dips slightly.
3. Fare-lock: a visible "hold this fare 10 min" CTA increases session-to-booking conversion, measurable via reduced multi-tab bounce in session replay.
Success metrics to track
Stage-to-stage conversion (especially Results→Details), overall search-to-confirmation conversion, average time-in-funnel, fare-review abandonment rate segmented by add-on opt-in vs. opt-out cohort.
Reusable insight
A funnel chart only shows where users leave — the RICE-worthy work is pairing each drop-off with a testable behavioral reason (anchoring, default bias, form fatigue), not recommending "reduce friction" everywhere.

T11

Flight Search & Price Comparison Engine

Core system & companies
Search Systems — Google Flights, Skyscanner, Kayak. The engine that turns "Delhi to Bangkok, next week" into a ranked, comparable set of fares across airlines and OTAs.
What PMs build here
Meta-search (aggregating other platforms' inventory and referring out) vs. direct search (querying and selling your own inventory) — a fundamentally different product and monetization model from a booking system, even though the search box looks identical to a user.
Key concepts to learn
1. Meta-search vs. OTA economics — Skyscanner/Kayak don't sell tickets, they refer (CPC or referral fee); a booking platform earns margin on the fare. Same search box, opposite business model.
2. Real-time fare staleness — GDS/airline APIs rate-limit and cache; a shown fare can go stale between search and click-through. "Fare no longer available" is a core UX problem here, not an edge case.
3. Ranking trade-offs — cheapest-for-user vs. highest-referral-fee-for-business; how a meta-search product decides what "best result" even means.
4. Flexible-date search (±3 days, whole-month calendar view) as a distinct product surface from single-date search, with a very different backend query pattern.
Questions a future teardown should answer
How does Google Flights' ranking differ from Skyscanner's? What's the actual fare-staleness recovery flow when a user clicks through to a dead price? How is a meta-search product's North Star (clicks-to-partner) different from an OTA's (completed bookings)?
Skills demonstrated
Marketplace/aggregator business models, ranking algorithm trade-offs, real-time data staleness handling — distinct from the single-inventory booking-flow thinking in T1.

Core system & companies
Travel Distribution Systems — Amadeus IT Group, Sabre Corporation. The B2B plumbing that makes every other system on this list possible — the GDS is what an OTA's search actually queries behind the scenes.
