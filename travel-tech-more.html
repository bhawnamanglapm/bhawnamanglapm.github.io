Flight Booking Product Teardown

CIRCLES method HEART framework (Google) OKR-aligned recommendation RICE + MoSCoW Business model & risk analysis

Frameworks used: CIRCLES, RICE, MoSCoW, HEART (Google), OKRs — written analysis, no build.

C — Comprehend the Situation

The clarifying questions I'd ask before touching the product: which platform (web vs. app: both), domestic or international (domestic only), and an existing product or a new one (existing, mature product). Scope: MakeMyTrip's flight search → booking flow specifically, not hotels or holidays, narrower on purpose so the analysis goes deep on one flow instead of shallow across three.

Business model: the flight vertical monetizes through airline commissions/overrides, a convenience fee layered on the base fare, and ancillary revenue (seat selection, insurance, baggage). That matters here specifically. Add-on pre-selection and the late-stage fee reveal found below aren't accidental UX oversights; they're revenue mechanisms, and removing them has a real, quantifiable cost. See Risks & Trade-offs below.

North Star Metric (company level): completed bookings per unique searcher, which normalizes for the high browsing-only traffic that never intends to book. This teardown is scoped to moving that metric one funnel stage at a time, starting with the stage that leaks hardest.

I — Identify the Customer

Three personas span the segmentation axes that actually predict booking behavior: price sensitivity, time pressure, and party complexity, not demographic axes like age or city, which don't:

  • "Value Rakesh" (28, IT professional, 4–5 domestic trips/yr): price-sensitive, compares 3+ apps before booking.
  • "Business Priya" (34, consulting manager): books last-minute, values reliability and rebooking flexibility over price.
  • "Family Planner Anita" (41): books for 4+ pax, needs group-seat clarity and abandons on unclear total-fare breakdowns.

R — Report the Customer's Needs

Journey: Home search widget → results list (filters/sort) → fare review (baggage/meal add-ons) → traveller details → payment → confirmation + "My Trips." Logged-out users are funnelled into account creation at payment, not upfront; that reduces search friction but adds a late-stage form-fill tax.

Retention loop: "My Trips" is a lifecycle retention engine wearing a post-booking utility tab as a disguise. It's why a user reopens the app at all after booking (rebooking, cancellation, web check-in), and why Business Priya's reliance on it post-confirmation (see the funnel map below) reads as a commercial signal rather than a UX nicety: it's the retention mechanism the acquisition funnel below actually feeds into.

Needs uncovered, mapped to the journey above:

  • Trust/transparency need: fare inflation between search-result price and fare-review price (taxes/fees revealed late) erodes trust; add-on pre-selection (insurance, seat) defaults to "on," reading as a dark pattern at checkout.
  • Time-efficiency need: filter fatigue (12+ dimensions, no smart default) and no persistent price comparison across dates force users to manually reopen search in a new tab.
  • Clarity/control need: baggage policy buried a tap deep, a top cause of post-booking support contact.

Funnel: Search → Results → Fare Review → Traveller Info → Payment → Confirmation. The biggest modeled drop-off sits between Results and Fare Review (price-shock from add-on fees: modeled at ~38% conversion in the Booking Funnel Analysis (T8)), with a second leak between Traveller Info and Payment (form fatigue + OTP retry friction).

C — Cut Through Prioritization

Of the three personas, I'd prioritize Value Rakesh's need first. Price-sensitive comparison shoppers are both the largest segment of OTA search traffic and the segment most exposed to the single biggest funnel leak above: the fare-review price shock hits a price-anchored searcher hardest. Business Priya's reliability need and Family Planner Anita's group-booking complexity are real, but lower-frequency, worth a later cycle rather than this one. Everything below is scoped to fixing Rakesh's need first.

L — List Solutions

  1. All-in fare display at the results level: kill the late-stage price reveal.
  2. Opt-in (not opt-out) add-ons at fare review.
  3. Persistent "compare dates" strip on results instead of reopening search.
  4. Baggage/meal policy inline on the fare card, not a tap away.
  5. One-tap "hold my seat 15 min" for indecisive high-intent users.
  6. Smart filter defaults ranked by price + duration + layover, not price alone.
  7. Payment retry with saved card + biometric re-auth to cut payment-stage drop-off.
  8. Proactive delay-risk badge on results (feeds the Flight Delay Dashboard (T5)).
  9. Group-booking mode for 4+ pax with a shared itinerary link.
  10. Post-booking "manage trip" nudges (web check-in, gate-change alerts) via push.

Risks & Trade-offs

Every recommendation above has a real cost. Naming it here, before launch, not after:

  • All-in fare display: the honest total can look higher than a competitor's headline price at the results stage, hurting click-through from price-comparison meta-search and paid search ads. The fix that builds trust downstream may cost impressions upstream.
  • Opt-in add-ons: directly reduces ancillary revenue per booking (insurance/seat attach rate). This is the business-model trade-off named in Comprehend, made concrete: it needs a revenue-per-booking floor agreed with Finance before shipping, beyond a simple UX sign-off.
  • Baggage/meal policy inline: minor engineering cost, no material revenue risk. The lowest-risk item on the list, consistent with its RICE ranking.
  • Delay-risk badge: risks eroding trust in a different direction if the underlying prediction (see the Flight Delay Dashboard, T5) isn't well-calibrated. A wrong badge is worse than no badge.

Mitigation: ship all-in pricing and opt-in add-ons behind the A/B test already scoped in the Booking Funnel Analysis (T8), with revenue-per-booking as a guardrail metric alongside conversion, not conversion alone. A win on Fare Review conversion that quietly craters ancillary revenue is a loss dressed up as a win.

S — Summarize Recommendation

Ship all-in fare display and opt-in add-ons first: RICE 13.5 and 14.4, the two highest-scored and lowest-effort items on the list. Together they remove the exact price-shock that costs the funnel its single biggest leak, for the segment (Rakesh) chosen in the Cut step above. Everything else on this list sequences behind those two.

Success Metrics: the HEART Framework

Using Google's own HEART framework (Rodden et al.) instead of a single catch-all "engagement" number:

  • Happiness: CSAT specifically on "pricing clarity" (post-booking survey); NPS delta before/after the all-in pricing rollout.
  • Engagement: searches per session; % of sessions using the new compare-dates strip.
  • Adoption: % of eligible searchers who see and interact with the all-in fare display / opt-in add-on toggle in month one.
  • Retention: repeat booking rate within 90 days, segmented by whether a user's first booking hit the old opt-out add-on flow or the new opt-in one.
  • Task success: Results→Fare Review conversion (the primary funnel-leak metric, North Star for this cycle), search-to-confirmation completion rate, time-to-book.

Tied to an OKR

Objective: Remove price-shock and trust friction from fare review for price-sensitive searchers.

  • KR1: Increase Results→Fare Review conversion from the ~38% modeled baseline (see Booking Funnel Analysis, T8) to 50%+ within one quarter.
  • KR2: Reduce "hidden fees" CSAT verbatims by 50%.
  • KR3: Ship both P1/Must recommendations this quarter, validated against the A/B hypotheses already scoped in T8.

T11

Flight Search & Price Comparison Engine

The System

Search Systems — Google Flights, Skyscanner, Kayak. The engine that turns "Delhi to Bangkok, next week" into a ranked, comparable set of fares across airlines and OTAs. What PMs build here is meta-search (aggregating other platforms' inventory and referring out) vs. direct search (querying and selling your own inventory): a fundamentally different product and monetization model from a booking system, even though the search box looks identical to a user.

Key Concepts

  • Meta-search vs. OTA economics: Skyscanner/Kayak don't sell tickets, they refer (CPC or referral fee); a booking platform earns margin on the fare. Same search box, opposite business model.
  • Real-time fare staleness: GDS/airline APIs rate-limit and cache; a shown fare can go stale between search and click-through. "Fare no longer available" is a core UX problem here, not an edge case.
  • Ranking trade-offs: cheapest-for-user vs. highest-referral-fee-for-business; how a meta-search product decides what "best result" even means.
  • Flexible-date search (±3 days, whole-month calendar view) as a distinct product surface from single-date search, with a very different backend query pattern.

Open Questions

How does Google Flights' ranking differ from Skyscanner's? What's the actual fare-staleness recovery flow when a user clicks through to a dead price? How is a meta-search product's North Star (clicks-to-partner) different from an OTA's (completed bookings)?

Skills Demonstrated

Marketplace/aggregator business models, ranking algorithm trade-offs, real-time data staleness handling — distinct from the single-inventory booking-flow thinking in T1.

T12

Travel Marketplace Platform

The System

Marketplace Systems — Airbnb, GetYourGuide. Two-sided platforms connecting hosts/suppliers with guests/travellers, where trust and liquidity are the core problems, not booking UX. What PMs build here is everything a single-inventory OTA (T1) doesn't need: supply-side onboarding, trust and safety systems, and a matching/ranking model that has to weigh host quality alongside price.

Key Concepts

  • Two-sided liquidity: no guests without hosts, no hosts without guests. Which side do you subsidize first, and how do you measure "liquidity" as a real metric, not a slogan?
  • Trust & safety as core product: reviews, ID verification, host guarantees, guest screening — the actual mechanism that makes a stranger's home bookable at all.
  • Marketplace ranking: host quality signals vs. price vs. availability vs. novelty; very different from ranking a fixed airline-fare inventory.
  • Take-rate design: host fee vs. guest fee vs. blended, and the transparency trade-offs each creates in the checkout UX.

Open Questions

How does Airbnb's search ranking balance host quality vs. price vs. novelty? What's the trust-building flow a first-time host actually goes through? How does GetYourGuide's supply-side onboarding differ from Airbnb's, given smaller/more numerous suppliers?

Skills Demonstrated

Two-sided marketplace dynamics, trust & safety product design, supply-side (not just demand-side) product thinking.

T13

Ride-Sharing & Mobility Booking

The System

Mobility Systems — Uber, BlaBlaCar. Real-time matching between riders and drivers/co-travellers, dynamic pricing, and ETA-driven trust — a live-match product, not a browse-and-book one. What PMs build here is dispatch/matching algorithms and pricing mechanics that respond in seconds, not the search-and-compare flows every other system on this list uses.

Key Concepts

  • Real-time supply/demand matching: the product surface is a live match, not a list of results to browse; latency itself is a feature.
  • Surge/dynamic pricing: the most studied and most controversial pricing mechanism in travel-tech; balancing driver-supply incentive against rider price sensitivity and perceived fairness.
  • ETA accuracy as trust: a wrong ETA is a broken promise, not a UX nitpick; it's the primary trust mechanic in a product with no browsable inventory to build confidence from.
  • Marketplace vs. peer-to-peer: Uber owns dispatch and pricing outright; BlaBlaCar is closer to a trust-and-coordination layer over independently-arranged car-share trips.

Open Questions

How would you redesign surge pricing to reduce rider anger while preserving driver supply incentives? What's BlaBlaCar's trust model for a multi-hour shared car ride, versus Uber's for a 10-minute trip?

Skills Demonstrated

Real-time marketplace/dispatch systems, dynamic pricing design, trust mechanics in a zero-inventory-browse product.

T14

Tours & Activities Marketplace

The System

Experience Marketplace — Viator, Klook. Discovery and booking for bookable experiences (tours, tickets, activities): a catalog/inventory problem more than a real-time-matching one. What PMs build here is supply-side tooling for thousands of small local operators (not a handful of airlines), and the cross-sell integration point where "book a flight" surfaces "things to do at your destination."

Key Concepts

  • Perishable inventory at small scale: an unsold tour slot today is gone forever, the same yield-management problem as an airline seat, but per-operator volume is tiny compared to an airline.
  • Long-tail supply: thousands of small local operators instead of a handful of airlines; supply-side tooling and quality control look completely different from T1's world.
  • Instant confirmation vs. request-to-book: some experiences confirm immediately, others need the operator to manually accept; a real trust/UX trade-off, not just an engineering detail.
  • Cross-sell placement: where in a booking flow (like T1's) should experience upsells actually appear, and does that belong to this system or the booking system it's bolted onto?

Open Questions

How does Klook's supply-side operator tooling differ from a single-inventory OTA's? Where in the funnel (see T8) should an experience upsell actually be placed, and what does it cannibalize if placed too early?

Skills Demonstrated

Long-tail supply management, perishable-inventory yield thinking at small scale, cross-sell/attach-rate product design.

Travel Discovery & Reviews Platform

The System

Travel Discovery Systems — Tripadvisor, Yelp. Pre-booking inspiration and trust — reviews, rankings, and content that influence a decision made and completed somewhere else entirely. What PMs build here is a non-transactional product: most Tripadvisor traffic never books on Tripadvisor. The PM problem is monetizing influence, not completing a purchase.

Key Concepts

  • Monetizing a non-transactional product: referral/ads revenue instead of booking margin; a fundamentally different business model than every OTA on this list.
  • Review fraud & fake-review detection: the core trust problem: how do you know a 5-star review is real, without over-flagging genuine negative ones?
  • Content ranking vs. e-commerce ranking: a "best restaurant" ranking has no purchase event forcing a decision; different signals matter than in a booking-funnel ranking (T8).
  • SEO as the primary growth channel: discovery platforms live or die on organic search traffic in a way booking platforms (T1) don't have to.

Open Questions

How would you detect and penalize fake reviews without over-flagging real negative ones? What's Tripadvisor's actual North Star metric if it isn't bookings?

Skills Demonstrated

Non-transactional business models, trust/fraud detection systems, content ranking, SEO-driven growth strategy.

TL;DR

"Wayfare AI": a conversational trip-planning assistant that turns "plan me a trip" into a bookable, weather- and budget-aware itinerary in one session. MVP scope: single-destination domestic trips only, 3–7 days. Ships itinerary generation, a budget optimizer, and weather-aware sequencing; explicitly does not attempt multi-city routing, group itineraries, or live disruption reflow in v1; see Goals & Non-Goals below for why.

Problem Statement

Planning a multi-day trip today means 8–12 open tabs (flights, hotels, blogs, weather, maps) and hours of manual cross-referencing. Users abandon planning mid-way and either overpay on a generic package or under-plan and lose time on-trip re-deciding.

Goals & Non-Goals

Goals (v1):

  • G1: Cut time-to-first-itinerary to under 90 seconds for a first-time planner.
  • G2: Convert at least one booked leg per generated itinerary, at a target rate set post-launch (the North Star, below).
  • G3: Let a budget-conscious traveller swap any single item without manually recalculating the rest of the plan.

Non-Goals (explicitly out of scope for v1):

  • Multi-city or multi-destination routing.
  • Shared or group itineraries.
  • Live disruption reflow: needs a flight-status API integration not yet built; sequenced for v2 once MVP itinerary quality is validated, not because it's unimportant (it's Reactive Rhea's entire use case, see below).
  • Replacing a travel agent for complex international multi-leg trips: this is a single-destination planning aid, not a full-service booking replacement.

Users & Use Cases

  • "First-timer Farah": plans 1 international trip/yr, wants 2–3 easy choices, not raw options.
  • "Optimizer Omar": books 6+ trips/yr, wants full control and only trusts AI suggestions he can see the reasoning behind.
  • "Reactive Rhea": mid-trip, needs the plan to adapt live when a flight delays or weather turns (her use case is explicitly a Non-Goal for v1; see above).

User stories:

  • As a first-time planner, I want to type "5 days in Vietnam under ₹60k" and get a full day-by-day itinerary, so I don't have to research every leg myself.
  • As a budget-conscious traveller, I want to see how each day's plan affects my total spend, so I can swap a paid activity for a free one without recalculating manually.
  • As a returning user, I want the assistant to remember my dietary and pace preferences, so I don't re-explain them every trip.
  • As a skeptical planner, I want to see why a restaurant or attraction was recommended, so I can trust or override the suggestion.

Design: Key Flows

  • Intent capture: single chat-style input + 3 quick-pick chips (budget band, trip length, vibe), not a 12-field form.
  • Itinerary review: day-by-day vertical timeline, each activity swappable via a "Why this?" expand showing rating/distance/price reasoning.
  • Budget bar: persistent sticky bar showing spend-to-date vs. target, updating live as items are swapped.
  • Book/hold: itinerary converts to a bookable checklist (flight, hotel, key activities) with per-leg deep links.

Success Metrics

North Star: itineraries that convert to at least one booked leg. Supporting: time-to-first-itinerary (target <90s, ties to G1), swap rate per itinerary (a trust signal on first-pass suggestion quality: a high swap rate means the first draft is missing the mark), budget-target adherence rate, D7 return rate for pre-trip re-edits.

Launch Plan

  • Phase 1 (MVP): single-destination domestic trips, 3–7 days. Itinerary generation + budget optimizer + weather sequencing only: the three P0 requirements.
  • Phase 2: multi-city routing + persistent preference memory, gated on Phase 1 hitting its swap-rate and time-to-itinerary targets. Routing multiple cities is premature if the single-city itinerary still isn't trusted.
  • Phase 3: live disruption reflow (Reactive Rhea's use case), gated on a flight-status API integration that doesn't exist yet.

Risks & Open Questions

Risk: hallucinated venue details (wrong hours, permanently closed) need a live data-freshness check; training-time accuracy alone won't catch them. Recommendation logic must stay auditable and explainable so it doesn't over-fit to popular/sponsored listings (same scoring discipline as the Hotel Recommendation Engine project, still on the roadmap).

Open questions this doc doesn't resolve:

  • Should the budget optimizer be allowed to suggest a different destination than requested if it saves >30% of budget, or does that override user intent in a way that breaks trust?
  • What's the fallback UX when no itinerary can be generated within the stated budget: degrade gracefully with a "closest match," or refuse and ask the user to adjust?
  • Does surfacing restaurant/attraction rankings require a sponsored-placement disclosure, and who owns that legal review before launch?

Why Trust Is the Real Problem

Generating an itinerary is the easy half. The real PM problem is the trust layer around it: the "why this?" reasoning, the swap-not-regenerate interaction, whatever lets a user hand over planning without feeling like they lost control of the trip.

Customer Support AI Copilot

Where the Volume Comes From

The support queries that pile up fastest at an OTA ("cancel my flight," "how much baggage can I bring," "can I change the travel date") are also the most repetitive and rule-governed. A human agent resolving them one ticket at a time is expensive and slow at OTA volumes; a plain FAQ chatbot that only answers questions can't actually act on a booking. The gap this project scopes is the one in between: an assistant that can both answer and execute (cancel a booking, initiate a refund, apply a fare rule), which is a materially different (and riskier) design problem than a Q&A bot, because it can now make a costly, sometimes irreversible mistake on its own.

Scope & Non-Goals

In scope: cancellations, refund recommendations, baggage-allowance questions, and date/name booking changes: the four query types responsible for most repetitive support volume. Explicitly routed to a human, never handled autonomously: visa legal advice (a compliance and liability question, not a support one; the bot can point to the airline/embassy's own documentation but never advise), disputes and fraud claims, and any refund above a fixed autonomous-action ceiling (see Guardrails below).

Where Today's Industry Falls Short

Before designing the flows, it's worth naming the pattern this design is deliberately not repeating. Travel and OTA support bots in production today tend to fail in one of four recurring ways. This is a pattern visible across the category, not a claim about any specific company's product:

  • Answer-only, can't act: most bots can tell you the cancellation policy but can't actually cancel anything; the moment a user needs an action taken, they're back in a queue for a human, which defeats the point of automating the repetitive cases in the first place.
  • Black-box numbers: the few bots that do quote a refund or fee rarely show the rule behind it, so a user has no way to check it's right, which is the same trust erosion the fare-shock problem in the Flight Booking Teardown (T1) causes at the booking stage.
  • Silent or absent confirmation: bots that can act either skip a confirmation step entirely (risky) or are so conservative they add friction to low-risk actions too (annoying): few calibrate the gate to the actual risk of the specific action, the way Flow 1 vs. Flow 2 do below.
  • Context loss at handoff: "please repeat your issue" after being passed to a human is one of the most common support complaints in the category; the bot's understanding of the problem evaporates the moment escalation happens.

Flow 2: Baggage Policy Question (lowest-risk flow)

The contrast case: a pure information lookup with no action taken and nothing to confirm, so it needs none of Flow 1's guardrails.

  • User: "How much check-in baggage do I get on my booking?"
  • Agent (intent: baggage_query, 0.99 confidence) → tool call: fetch fare rules for the booking's specific fare class → "Your Economy Saver fare includes 15kg check-in. Extra baggage is ₹500/kg if you add it now, or ₹800/kg at the airport — want me to add extra baggage to this booking?"

Fully autonomous end-to-end: answered and resolved the likely follow-up (add baggage) without a human, because nothing here is irreversible or high-value enough to need a confirmation gate.

Flow 3: Booking Date Change (medium-risk flow)

Sits between the two above: it's an action, so pure information-lookup rules don't apply, but a correctable one, so it gets a lighter version of Flow 1's gate: confirm the fare difference, but no separate human-escalation trigger unless the fare-difference lookup itself fails. Intent: change_booking_date → tool call: check seat availability + fare difference on new date → agent states the fare difference and change fee → single confirmation → executes change_booking() and emails the updated ticket.

Agent Architecture

Four layers, in order: (1) Intent router: classifies the incoming message into one of the in-scope intents above, or "unclassified" → human. (2) Tool-calling layer: each intent maps to a fixed set of callable tools (PNR lookup, refund_policy_engine, fare-difference calculator, cancel_booking, issue_refund, change_booking): the agent can only call tools on this allow-list, it cannot invent new actions. (3) Confirmation gate: any tool call that is irreversible or moves money (cancel_booking, issue_refund) requires an explicit user confirmation turn before executing, never fires on the first pass. (4) Escalation router: triggers on low intent-confidence (<0.85), a tool call failing twice, explicit user frustration signals, or any query touching a hard non-goal (visa advice, disputes): handed to a human agent with the full conversation transcript attached, not restarted from zero.

Guardrails

This is the part of the design that actually differentiates a support copilot from a support chatbot, and the part a scope doc for an agent that can take real actions can't skip:

  • Autonomous-action ceiling: the agent may autonomously execute cancellations/refunds up to a fixed rupee ceiling; above it, the same flow runs but ends in a human-approval step instead of auto-executing.
  • Confirmation before irreversible actions: never optional, shown in Flow 1 above; a wrong autonomous cancellation is far more costly to trust than a wrong autonomous baggage-fee quote.
  • No autonomous visa/legal advice: the intent router treats this as a hard non-goal, not a confidence threshold; it escalates every time, regardless of how confident the model is in an answer.
  • Full audit trail: every tool call the agent executes, conversations included, is logged with the triggering intent, confidence score, and outcome, reviewable by support ops, the same operational-audit discipline as the PCMS payout reconciliation work (see Technical Execution, T27).

Evaluation Approach

Deflection rate alone is a misleading headline metric here: a bot can "deflect" a ticket by giving an answer that satisfies the user in the moment but is wrong, which shows up as a good deflection number and a bad outcome. Evaluation needs three lenses, not one: resolution accuracy (a sampled human audit of closed bot-handled tickets weighed against the bot's own confidence score), wrong-action rate: the safety metric, tracking autonomous actions a human reviewer would have executed differently, weighted far more heavily than any convenience metric, and escalation-appropriateness, checking the router escalates when it should as often as it deflects when it shouldn't.

Risks

The risk that actually matters isn't "the bot gives a bad answer": a Q&A chatbot has that risk too, and it's recoverable. It's the bot takes a bad action: an autonomous cancellation or refund that shouldn't have happened costs real money and leaves a damaged booking behind, well past an unhelpful chat log's worth of harm. Every guardrail above exists to keep that specific failure mode's blast radius small and recoverable (confirmation gates, action ceilings, audit trails) rather than trying to prevent it outright, which no confidence threshold can fully guarantee.

What Carries Over From T18

The same lesson as the ChatGPT Memory Feature teardown (Feature Reverse Engineering, T18) shows up again here in a higher-stakes form: the hard PM problem in agentic AI isn't language understanding or intent classification (those are largely solved). It's designing the guardrail and escalation layer that keeps the agent's inevitable mistakes cheap and reversible instead of expensive and permanent.

The Gap Nobody's Closing

Three gaps stack on top of each other, and most tools only fix the first: nobody actually knows how work gets done. The real process lives in muscle memory across a dozen browser tabs, and documented SOPs are fiction. The insight dies in a slide, too: the distance from "here's an inefficiency" to someone actually building the fix is where most optimization effort quietly expires. And nobody proves it worked: optimization gets bought on ROI, but almost nobody closes the loop from "we changed X" to "it saved ₹Y / Z hours." Process-mining incumbents like Celonis and UiPath attack the first gap with heavy, IT-led, six-figure deployments, and stop there. Weft attacks all three, lightly, from the browser.

The Mechanism: Observer, Not Agent

Weft is a browser-side observer, not a tool that logs in for you. The user is already logged in; Weft watches while they work, with no credentials collected or stored, no form values, no page content, no screenshots. Anything textual that's retained passes a PII-redaction pass, and a privacy preview shows the user exactly what will be stored before it saves, closing a real hole found during a later security review, where structural ARIA roles on a data grid could otherwise leak on-screen content (a customer name in a table cell) even though form values were already masked.

The Actual Time Cost

A serious job application takes 6–8 hours of fragmented, error-prone manual work: decoding what a JD actually screens for, researching a company beyond stale memory, honestly assessing fit, tailoring a resume without breaking ATS parsing, building interview answers, and drafting several pieces of outreach copy, each from scratch, with no consistent quality bar. Generic AI chat makes this faster but not safer: it produces confident, plausible-sounding output that's frequently wrong in ways the candidate can't detect (invented metrics, inflated scope, interview answers built on facts not in the candidate's real history). In an interview, a fabricated claim doesn't fail quietly; it fails in front of the person deciding whether to hire you.

The Core Design Bet

The bet that shaped every architectural decision: a job-application AI system's most important property is evidence-traceability, well ahead of fluency: every claim it produces must resolve to something the candidate's actual resume states or a cited, real source, and the system must say "I don't have evidence for this" rather than invent something plausible. That single constraint drove everything from per-field null-ability in the schemas to a dedicated review agent whose entire job is catching violations of it.

Non-Fabrication as a Structural Constraint

Early on, non-fabrication was enforced entirely through system-prompt instructions ("never invent facts"). That's necessary but not sufficient: a model instructed to be helpful will still often fill a gap with something plausible rather than leave it empty, because empty feels unhelpful. The fix that actually held under review was structural: giving fields explicit null-ability and bracketed-placeholder conventions ([Recruiter Name]) as part of the schema itself, so "I don't have this" has a designated, expected place to go instead of competing with "make something up" as an implicit default.

Verification Approach

Every numeric claim across all 18 agents' sample outputs (ATS score arithmetic, gap-analysis row counts, skill-evidence confidence distributions, placeholder counts) was independently recomputed with a script against the underlying JSON, not just asserted in prose. Critic Agent's first real review pass came back almost entirely clean (14 of 15 agents approved or approved-with-minor-notes): read correctly as a red flag, not a green light, since a critic that always says "looks good" is indistinguishable from one that skipped the check entirely. The fix wasn't forcing it to find more issues artificially; it was giving it one concrete, mechanical task it couldn't fake: recompute every stated total against the numbers it's supposedly summarizing. That's what caught the one real bug in the project (Learning & Certification's stated total didn't match the sum of its own line items).

The Orchestration Layer

Planner, Critic, and Supervisor don't analyze resumes, JDs, or companies themselves; they reason about the other 15 agents. Their shared ground truth is AGENT_REGISTRY, a machine-readable catalog of every agent's required/optional inputs and real dependencies, built directly from how each agent is implemented, with every agent_id and depends_on reference in a Planner-generated plan programmatically checked against it before acceptance. This makes an entire class of hallucination (inventing an agent, or a dependency, that doesn't exist) structurally impossible rather than just less likely. Supervisor is the top-level entry point: it cross-checks Planner's execution plan against what actually ran, cross-checks every Critic-flagged issue against a named, specific fix rather than a general assurance, and renders a single go/no-go verdict while carrying forward every limitation the other 17 agents raised, instead of letting the verdict imply more confidence than the system actually earned.

What This Build Actually Surfaced

A shared registry beats trusting an agent's self-description. The tempting shortcut was describing the other agents inside each orchestrator's own system prompt, but that drifts the moment an agent's real required fields change and nobody updates three separate prompt descriptions; one machine-readable source of truth doesn't drift. Two-phase research beats one-phase "search and format": combining "search the web" and "structure the answer" into a single model call conflates two different failure modes (wrong facts vs. wrong JSON), so splitting them means a review can tell which one actually broke. And "insufficient evidence" has to be a first-class schema value, not a prompt suggestion; that's the lesson underneath the core design bet itself.

What Actually Made This Hard

Getting one agent to produce good output turned out to be the easy part. The hard part of building a trustworthy multi-agent system is designing the layer that keeps 18 agents honest about what they don't know, and makes that layer's own claims checkable rather than just plausible. Verification-by-recomputation, structural null-ability, and a shared dependency registry are three different tools solving the same underlying problem: trust that has to be earned by the system's design, not asserted in its prompts.

Why Nobody Acts on This Today

An OTA or airline can't act on delay risk it hasn't aggregated. A single flight-status page tells one traveller their flight is late; nothing rolls that up into the pattern a PM actually needs: which routes are structurally unreliable, which airlines run tighter operations, and when in the calendar or the day risk spikes. This project builds that rollup, answering the three questions a travel-tech PM would actually be asked before shipping something like the "delay-risk badge" recommended in the Flight Booking Teardown (T1): which routes delay most, how airlines differ, and how season and time of day shape delay risk.

My Approach

The honest constraint first: this was built in an environment that can't reach DGCA, US BTS, or Kaggle to pull a real historical dataset. Rather than present scraped-looking numbers that weren't actually real, I built a modeled dataset instead and said so directly on the dashboard: 8,000 flights generated from parameters I chose and documented: fictional airline names (so no delay number is ever attributed to a real, identifiable carrier), real Indian city-pair routes (airport congestion at Delhi/Mumbai/ Bengaluru and effects like North Indian winter fog are public aviation-industry knowledge, not a claim about any company), and additive delay effects for route congestion, season, and time-of-day. Delay minutes are drawn from a gamma distribution, not a normal one: real delays are right-skewed with a hard floor at zero, and gamma is bounded at zero and long-tailed by construction, which a normal distribution isn't. The first pass came out at a 48.9% on-time rate (implausibly low for any real airline), so I re-tuned the distribution parameters until the fleet-wide numbers matched a plausible real-world range, while keeping the underlying patterns (winter fog, monsoon, evening cascade) intact. Methodology: the calculation, step by step

For every one of the 8,000 simulated flights:

  • Draw a base delay from Gamma(shape=1.3, scale=3.2): mean ≈ 4.2 minutes, right-skewed.
  • Multiply by that flight's airline reliability index (a single fixed number per airline, see table below), the only airline-level effect in the model, kept deliberately simple so differences between the fictional carriers stay modest rather than dramatic.
  • Multiply by a time-of-day multiplier (0.90 Early Morning → 1.55 Night) to model how delays cascade later into an operating day.
  • Add a fixed route congestion term (see table below), scaled by 0.35, a stand-in for how much busier Delhi/Mumbai/Bengaluru are than smaller airports.
  • If the route is monsoon-sensitive and the month falls in Jun–Sep, add an extra Gamma(1.6, 5.0) term (mean ≈ 8 min).
  • If the route is fog-sensitive and the month is Dec/Jan, add an extra Gamma(1.8, 6.0) term (mean ≈ 10.8 min), amplified 1.3× for Early Morning departures, since fog is worst at dawn.
  • Cancellation is decided separately, as its own probability: a 0.6% base rate, +1.8pp if monsoon-exposed that month, +2.4pp if fog-exposed that month. If cancelled, delay is zeroed; a cancellation is a different outcome from "a very late flight," not the same thing at the extreme end.
  • A flight counts as delayed if its final delay exceeds 15 minutes (the standard on-time threshold) and it wasn't cancelled.

A Different Question Than the Delay Dashboard

Methodology: the calculation, step by step

For every one of the 6,000 simulated flights, in order:

  • On-time performance: starts from a base probability of 90%, reduced by that hub's congestion index (Delhi and Kolkata run busiest), then reduced further by −22pp if the hub is fog-sensitive and the month is Dec/Jan, or −14pp if monsoon-sensitive and the month is Jun–Sep.
  • Cancellation: decided separately as its own probability (base 0.6%, +hub congestion, +weather exposure). A cancelled flight is always counted as not on-time, never the reverse.
  • Load factor: drawn from a normal distribution around a base of 79%, shifted up in the Oct–Nov festive season and down in the Jan–Feb low season and at monsoon-exposed hubs during monsoon months.
  • Revenue: ticket revenue = passengers × a per-hub base fare × a seasonal fare multiplier; ancillary revenue (bags, seats, meals) added per passenger on top. Both zero for a cancelled flight.
  • Complaints: drawn from a weighted distribution that shifts hard toward more complaints when the flight was delayed, and harder still when it was cancelled — the one deliberate correlation this generator exists to demonstrate.
  • Baggage mishandling: one independent per-bag draw, rate rising with hub congestion and on any day operations went irregular (delayed or cancelled).

A data-quality decision worth naming: the dataset tracks two different passenger counts — passengers_expected (a demand estimate, computed the same way regardless of outcome) and passengers_boarded (zero for a cancelled flight, by definition). Load factor and revenue are correctly built on boarded passengers, since those only exist for flights that actually flew. My first pass used boarded passengers as the denominator for the complaints-rate metric too, which made cancelled flights show zero complaints per 1,000 passengers: the exact opposite of reality, purely because a cancelled flight boards nobody. I caught this by sanity-checking the output before building the chart, not after — the reliability-vs-complaints chart on the dashboard would otherwise have shown cancellations as the "safest" outcome for a customer, which is backwards. Complaints are rated against expected passengers instead: the people actually affected by the flight, whether it flew or not. This is the same kind of judgment call as excluding cancelled flights before averaging delay minutes in the sibling dashboard — a metric's denominator has to match what the metric is actually claiming to measure, and getting it wrong doesn't throw an error, it just silently produces a plausible-looking wrong number.

Why these specific numbers: not derived from any real source, chosen and then checked against public ballpark ranges for plausibility (domestic OTP typically 70–88%, load factor typically 75–85%, baggage mishandling typically 4–8 per 1,000 bags industry-wide). The fleet-wide numbers landed at 77.9% on-time, 78.9% load factor, and 5.4 bags mishandled per 1,000 — all inside those ranges. What this deliberately doesn't model: real distributional parameters fit from a published dataset (none was reachable from this environment), or knock-on delay (one late inbound aircraft delaying that same tail number's next departure) — a real ops model would need that, and it's a materially harder problem than this exercise scopes to.

Key Findings

  • 77.9% on-time fleet-wide, 78.9% average load factor, 1.2% cancellation rate, ₹6,38,860 average revenue per flight.
  • Bengaluru is the most reliable hub (83.3% on-time), Mumbai the least (74.6%): the two hubs with no modeled weather exposure sit at the top, the two with monsoon exposure sit at the bottom.

Root-Cause Reasoning

Three behavioral mechanisms explain most of the Results → Details leak:

  • Anchoring bias: once a user sees a low headline fare, any upward revision reads as deception, regardless of accuracy.
  • Add-on defaults: pre-selected insurance/seat fees compound the price-shock instead of being clearly separable.

  • Make add-ons opt-in with one clear toggle, not a pre-ticked line item.
  • Add a 10-minute fare-lock CTA on the details page for indecisive users.
  • Autofill/remember traveller info for repeat and return-trip bookings.
  • Auto-retry payment with the saved method before falling back to full re-entry on a gateway timeout.

Test Plan

  • All-in pricing: showing all-in fare at results increases Results→Details conversion by removing price-shock, possibly at a small cost to search-engine click-through.
  • Opt-in add-ons: switching seat/insurance from pre-selected to opt-in increases Details→Traveller Info conversion, even if average order value dips slightly.
  • Fare-lock: a visible "hold this fare 10 min" CTA increases session-to-booking conversion, measurable via reduced multi-tab bounce in session replay.

Stage-to-stage conversion (especially Results→Details), overall search-to-confirmation conversion, average time-in-funnel, and fare-review abandonment rate segmented by add-on opt-in vs. opt-out cohort.

A Real T-SQL Dialect Bug, and the Fix

These 4 queries were first written and verified against SQLite for a browser-based version of this project, then ported to T-SQL for SQL Server (FORMAT() instead of strftime(), DATEDIFF() instead of julianday()). Three of the four ported cleanly on the first run. The running-cumulative-signups query didn't:

Msg 8729, Level 16: ORDER BY list of RANGE window frame has total size of 8000 bytes. Largest size supported is 900 bytes.

The cause: FORMAT() returns nvarchar(4000) regardless of how short the actual string is, and SQL Server's default window-frame type (RANGE) caps its ORDER BY column at 900 bytes for that reason. The fix was one clause, not a rewrite — swapping the implicit RANGE frame for an explicit ROWS UNBOUNDED PRECEDING frame (the semantically correct frame for a running total anyway):

SUM(new_customers) OVER (ORDER BY month ROWS UNBOUNDED PRECEDING) AS cumulative_customers

The Feature

Duolingo's Streak: the daily-use counter that resets on a missed day. The user problem: learners forget, feel guilty, then quit. The business problem: D30 retention collapses without an intervention loop. Streak exists because loss aversion outperforms achievement motivation as a retention lever.

User Flow & Workflow

Complete at least one lesson before the user's local day rolls over → the streak counter increments and the flame icon updates → as the day-end window approaches with no lesson yet done, a reminder notification fires. Under the hood: streak state is per-user and evaluated against local midnight, not server time, the single detail that makes "what counts as today" a genuinely nontrivial edge case (see below). "Streak Freeze" can be pre-equipped as an item to auto-preserve the streak through one missed day; "Streak Repair" lets a user restore a broken streak after the fact, monetized alongside the free-currency ("gems") economy and the paid subscription tier.

Assumptions & Edge Cases

The design bets that loss aversion (not wanting to lose an accumulated number) beats pure achievement-seeking as a daily-return driver, and that a single short lesson is low enough friction to sustain the habit. Real edge cases this creates: timezone travel shifts the local-midnight boundary mid-streak; offline lesson completions have to sync before that boundary or the streak silently breaks; very long-running streaks (some users publicly report 1,000+ days) turn loss aversion into extreme lock-in, which is a retention asset but also a support-ticket and goodwill liability the moment a bug breaks one.

Failure & Abuse Scenarios

The most-discussed failure mode: users protect the streak with the fastest, easiest lesson available rather than one that reflects real learning progress: the metric gets gamed against the product's actual goal. The anxiety side can also overcorrect: a user who fears an inevitable eventual miss disengages entirely rather than risk it, which is the opposite of the intended effect. Streak Freeze mitigates the anxiety but introduces its own abuse case: it can become a crutch that quietly reduces real daily engagement while the visible number keeps climbing.

Trade-offs & Redesign

The "streak freeze" mechanic is the interesting abuse case here: it normalizes breaks while still keeping users inside the ecosystem. But the feature creates user anxiety alongside motivation; it's not a free win. A redesign worth considering: surface a visible "grace period" upfront to reduce that anxiety while preserving the retention hook.

Scope

Redesign a loyalty program for an OTA or airline: the same lifecycle-design problem every points program eventually hits: it drives initial sign-up, then stops driving behavior once a user stalls mid-tier.

Current Pain Points

Redemption friction: points that are easy to earn but hard to actually spend (blackout dates, limited reward inventory) train users to stop valuing them, which defeats the program's purpose. Tier stagnation: a user who reaches a mid-tier and realizes the next tier is unreachable within a normal booking cadence disengages entirely rather than working toward it. Opaque earning rules: when users can't predict what a booking will actually earn them, the program stops functioning as a behavioral lever at the moment of booking, which is the only moment that matters.

Redesign: Earning, Redemption & Gamification

Earning: shift part of the accrual from pure spend-based points to behavior-based points (completing a review, booking during a shoulder season the business wants to fill, or booking further in advance) so the program can be tuned toward business-value behavior instead of raw transaction volume. Redemption: guarantee a minimum real-value redemption path (even if less attractive than a rare premium reward) so points always feel spendable, closing the "earn but can't use" trust gap. Gamification: a visible streak for consecutive-quarter bookings (the same loss-aversion mechanic as Duolingo's Streak, see Feature Reverse Engineering above, applied to a much lower-frequency behavior); milestone tiers with a clearly visible "X bookings to next tier" progress indicator instead of an opaque point threshold; occasional surprise-and-delight unlocks (an unexpected small perk) to reward loyalty outside the predictable earn/redeem loop, which research on variable reward schedules suggests sustains engagement better than a purely predictable one.

KPIs

Repeat booking rate within the program (the core retention signal), tier upgrade rate (whether the redesigned progress visibility actually moves people, separate from whether points simply accrue), redemption rate (whether the "always spendable" fix actually closes the trust gap), and, as a guardrail, cost-per-loyalty-booking, since a redesign that drives repeat bookings purely by giving away margin is a loss dressed up as a win.

The Template

A PRD structured as seven fields (problem statement, user stories, business goals, requirements, acceptance criteria, success metrics, launch plan), the same structure reused across the Travel PRD Portfolio (T10) and the AI Trip Planner PRD (T4). Seven fields, not twelve: every extra section is a section a stakeholder skims past, and a skimmed section is worse than a missing one because it creates the illusion the question got answered.

Positioning

"A PRD reads like a spec doc. It works like a decision memo in disguise." The tell is which section gets read first: nobody opens a PRD to find out how a button works; they open it to find out whether the thing is worth building at all, which is a Goals and Requirements question, not an Acceptance Criteria one. Write the doc in that order and most of the actual disagreement in a review happens on page one, where it's cheap to argue about, instead of surfacing three weeks into build.

Where This Template Breaks

It's built for a feature with a defined shape: something that has a clear "done." It strains on genuinely open-ended, discovery-stage work, where forcing Acceptance Criteria this early just produces confident-sounding fiction. Swapping in a different template doesn't fix that; the honest fix is admitting the doc is a hypothesis memo at that stage, not a requirements doc, and labeling it that way instead of dressing it up as more certain than it is.
