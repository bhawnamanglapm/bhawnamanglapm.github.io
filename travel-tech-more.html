T8

Booking Funnel Analysis

Funnel scope
OTA flight booking funnel: Search → Results → Details (fare review) → Traveller Info → Payment → Confirmation.
Biggest drop-off
Results → Details (modeled ~62% loss) — the fare-review price reveal is the single largest funnel leak. Users who arrive price-anchored from the results page bounce once taxes, fees, and add-ons inflate the total.
Root-cause reasoning
1. Anchoring bias — once a user sees a low headline fare, any upward revision reads as deception, regardless of accuracy.
2. Add-on defaults — pre-selected insurance/seat fees compound the price-shock instead of being clearly separable.
3. No fare-lock — an indecisive user has no reason to stay on the page vs. reopening a new tab to compare.
Improvement ideas
1. Show all-in price (base + tax + fees) at the results stage, even at the cost of a slightly less eye-catching headline number.
2. Make add-ons opt-in with one clear toggle, not a pre-ticked line item.
3. Add a 10-minute fare-lock CTA on the details page for indecisive users.
4. Autofill/remember traveller info for repeat and return-trip bookings.
5. Auto-retry payment with the saved method before falling back to full re-entry on a gateway timeout.
A/B test hypotheses
1. All-in pricing: showing all-in fare at results increases Results→Details conversion by removing price-shock, possibly at a small cost to search-engine click-through.
2. Opt-in add-ons: switching seat/insurance from pre-selected to opt-in increases Details→Traveller Info conversion, even if average order value dips slightly.
3. Fare-lock: a visible "hold this fare 10 min" CTA increases session-to-booking conversion, measurable via reduced multi-tab bounce in session replay.
Success metrics to track
Stage-to-stage conversion (especially Results→Details), overall search-to-confirmation conversion, average time-in-funnel, fare-review abandonment rate segmented by add-on opt-in vs. opt-out cohort.
Reusable insight
A funnel chart only shows where users leave — the RICE-worthy work is pairing each drop-off with a testable behavioral reason (anchoring, default bias, form fatigue), not recommending "reduce friction" everywhere.

T11

Flight Search & Price Comparison Engine

Core system & companies
Search Systems — Google Flights, Skyscanner, Kayak. The engine that turns "Delhi to Bangkok, next week" into a ranked, comparable set of fares across airlines and OTAs.
What PMs build here
Meta-search (aggregating other platforms' inventory and referring out) vs. direct search (querying and selling your own inventory) — a fundamentally different product and monetization model from a booking system, even though the search box looks identical to a user.
Key concepts to learn
1. Meta-search vs. OTA economics — Skyscanner/Kayak don't sell tickets, they refer (CPC or referral fee); a booking platform earns margin on the fare. Same search box, opposite business model.
2. Real-time fare staleness — GDS/airline APIs rate-limit and cache; a shown fare can go stale between search and click-through. "Fare no longer available" is a core UX problem here, not an edge case.
3. Ranking trade-offs — cheapest-for-user vs. highest-referral-fee-for-business; how a meta-search product decides what "best result" even means.
4. Flexible-date search (±3 days, whole-month calendar view) as a distinct product surface from single-date search, with a very different backend query pattern.
Questions a future teardown should answer
How does Google Flights' ranking differ from Skyscanner's? What's the actual fare-staleness recovery flow when a user clicks through to a dead price? How is a meta-search product's North Star (clicks-to-partner) different from an OTA's (completed bookings)?
Skills demonstrated
Marketplace/aggregator business models, ranking algorithm trade-offs, real-time data staleness handling — distinct from the single-inventory booking-flow thinking in T1.

T12

Travel Marketplace Platform

Core system & companies
Marketplace Systems — Airbnb, GetYourGuide. Two-sided platforms connecting hosts/suppliers with guests/travellers, where trust and liquidity are the core problems, not booking UX.
What PMs build here
Everything a single-inventory OTA (T1) doesn't need: supply-side onboarding, trust and safety systems, and a matching/ranking model that has to weigh host quality alongside price.
Key concepts to learn
1. Two-sided liquidity — no guests without hosts, no hosts without guests. Which side do you subsidize first, and how do you measure "liquidity" as a real metric, not a slogan?
2. Trust & safety as core product — reviews, ID verification, host guarantees, guest screening — the actual mechanism that makes a stranger's home bookable at all.
3. Marketplace ranking — host quality signals vs. price vs. availability vs. novelty; very different from ranking a fixed airline-fare inventory.
4. Take-rate design — host fee vs. guest fee vs. blended, and the transparency trade-offs each creates in the checkout UX.
Questions a future teardown should answer
How does Airbnb's search ranking balance host quality vs. price vs. novelty? What's the trust-building flow a first-time host actually goes through? How does GetYourGuide's supply-side onboarding differ from Airbnb's, given smaller/more numerous suppliers?
Skills demonstrated
Two-sided marketplace dynamics, trust & safety product design, supply-side (not just demand-side) product thinking.

T13

Ride-Sharing & Mobility Booking

Core system & companies
Mobility Systems — Uber, BlaBlaCar. Real-time matching between riders and drivers/co-travellers, dynamic pricing, and ETA-driven trust — a live-match product, not a browse-and-book one.
What PMs build here
Dispatch/matching algorithms and pricing mechanics that respond in seconds, not the search-and-compare flows every other system on this list uses.
Key concepts to learn
1. Real-time supply/demand matching — the product surface is a live match, not a list of results to browse; latency itself is a feature.
2. Surge/dynamic pricing — the most studied and most controversial pricing mechanism in travel-tech; balancing driver-supply incentive against rider price sensitivity and perceived fairness.
3. ETA accuracy as trust — a wrong ETA is a broken promise, not a UX nitpick; it's the primary trust mechanic in a product with no browsable inventory to build confidence from.
4. Marketplace vs. peer-to-peer — Uber owns dispatch and pricing outright; BlaBlaCar is closer to a trust-and-coordination layer over independently-arranged car-share trips.
Questions a future teardown should answer
How would you redesign surge pricing to reduce rider anger while preserving driver supply incentives? What's BlaBlaCar's trust model for a multi-hour shared car ride, versus Uber's for a 10-minute trip?
Skills demonstrated
Real-time marketplace/dispatch systems, dynamic pricing design, trust mechanics in a zero-inventory-browse product.

T14

Tours & Activities Marketplace

Core system & companies
Experience Marketplace — Viator, Klook. Discovery and booking for bookable experiences (tours, tickets, activities) — a catalog/inventory problem more than a real-time-matching one.
What PMs build here
Supply-side tooling for thousands of small local operators (not a handful of airlines), and the cross-sell integration point where "book a flight" surfaces "things to do at your destination."
Key concepts to learn
1. Perishable inventory at small scale — an unsold tour slot today is gone forever, the same yield-management problem as an airline seat, but per-operator volume is tiny compared to an airline.
2. Long-tail supply — thousands of small local operators instead of a handful of airlines; supply-side tooling and quality control look completely different from T1's world.
3. Instant confirmation vs. request-to-book — some experiences confirm immediately, others need the operator to manually accept; a real trust/UX trade-off, not just an engineering detail.
4. Cross-sell placement — where in a booking flow (like T1's) should experience upsells actually appear, and does that belong to this system or the booking system it's bolted onto?
Questions a future teardown should answer
How does Klook's supply-side operator tooling differ from a single-inventory OTA's? Where in the funnel (see T8) should an experience upsell actually be placed, and what does it cannibalize if placed too early?
Skills demonstrated
Long-tail supply management, perishable-inventory yield thinking at small scale, cross-sell/attach-rate product design.

T15

Travel Discovery & Reviews Platform

Core system & companies
Travel Discovery Systems — Tripadvisor, Yelp. Pre-booking inspiration and trust — reviews, rankings, and content that influence a decision made and completed somewhere else entirely.
What PMs build here
A non-transactional product: most Tripadvisor traffic never books on Tripadvisor. The PM problem is monetizing influence, not completing a purchase.
Key concepts to learn
1. Monetizing a non-transactional product — referral/ads revenue instead of booking margin; a fundamentally different business model than every OTA on this list.
2. Review fraud & fake-review detection — the core trust problem: how do you know a 5-star review is real, without over-flagging genuine negative ones?
3. Content ranking vs. e-commerce ranking — a "best restaurant" ranking has no purchase event forcing a decision; different signals matter than in a booking-funnel ranking (T8).
4. SEO as the primary growth channel — discovery platforms live or die on organic search traffic in a way booking platforms (T1) don't have to.
Questions a future teardown should answer
How would you detect and penalize fake reviews without over-flagging real negative ones? What's Tripadvisor's actual North Star metric if it isn't bookings?
Skills demonstrated
Non-transactional business models, trust/fraud detection systems, content ranking, SEO-driven growth strategy.

T16

Travel Payments & Refunds System

Core system & companies
Travel Payments Systems — Stripe, Adyen. The payment infrastructure underneath every other system here — a single itinerary can touch 4+ currencies and vendors at once.
What PMs build here
Multi-currency, multi-method payment handling and refund/dispute resolution at travel-specific scale and complexity, not a generic checkout flow.
Key concepts to learn
1. Split payments & multi-vendor settlement — one trip booking might pay an airline, a hotel, and a local tour operator; who gets paid when, and who eats a refund when one leg cancels?
2. Chargeback & dispute handling — travel has uniquely high dispute rates (cancelled flights, no-show hotels) — a payments PM's core recurring headache.
3. Currency conversion & FX risk — who absorbs the exchange-rate movement between the booking date and the travel date, months later?
4. Refund policy as a product surface, not a policy doc — partial refunds, cash vs. credit, voucher expiry are UX decisions with real financial modeling behind each one.
Questions a future teardown should answer
How would you design a refund policy for a 3-vendor itinerary where only one vendor cancels? What's the actual dispute-resolution flow when a traveller claims non-delivery of a paid-for service?
Skills demonstrated
Payments infrastructure design, multi-party settlement logic, dispute/refund policy as product design — not just a support-team script.

T17

Airline/Hotel Distribution System (GDS)

Core system & companies
Travel Distribution Systems — Amadeus IT Group, Sabre Corporation. The B2B plumbing that makes every other system on this list possible — the GDS is what an OTA's search actually queries behind the scenes.
What PMs build here
B2B infrastructure for travel agencies and OTAs, not a consumer product — API reliability, partner SLAs, and integration timelines are the actual PM discipline, not UX polish.
Key concepts to learn
1. GDS as B2B infrastructure — the customer is a travel agency or OTA, not a traveller; a completely different PM discipline than every consumer-facing system above.
2. NDC (New Distribution Capability) — the airline industry's real, current push to bypass traditional GDS with direct API access; an active disruption to this exact business model, worth naming by name.
3. Inventory synchronization latency — a seat sold on one channel must be un-sold everywhere else within seconds, or the airline oversells.
4. Switching costs & lock-in — why airlines and agencies rarely change GDS providers; the actual moat this multi-decade business runs on.
Questions a future teardown should answer
Why is NDC a genuine threat to Amadeus/Sabre's business model, and how would a PM there respond? What does a GDS integration project actually look like from an OTA's engineering side (ties to T1's "technical execution" comfort)?
Skills demonstrated
B2B/infrastructure product management, API-as-product thinking, understanding a legacy moat well enough to know what threatens it.
